We'll delve into all the details shortly, but first, let's determine whether this style of coffee machine is a good fit for you. 

The DeLonghi Dedica is an espresso machine.

Some people view a coffee machine simply as a coffee machine, which isn't helped by the fact that manufacturers often use the terms “coffee machine” and “espresso machine” interchangeably.

If you want to drink filter coffee, for example, then you need a filter coffee machine.

However, if you do want to drink espresso and espresso-based drinks, such as cappuccino, latte, flat white, Americano, lungo, and so on, then all is good so far, as the Delonghi Dedica is an espresso machine.

The next thing to note is that the De'Longhi Dedica is a semi-automatic traditional espresso machine.

What this means is that there is more to using this machine than using a bean-to-cup coffee machine.

Bean-to-cup coffee machines are also espresso machines, but they feature a built-in grinder and handle most of the work for you. 

Standard bean-to-cup machines handle all the coffee aspects of the process for you, and you simply steam the milk using the provided steam wand. 

One-touch “cappuccino maker” or “super automatic” bean-to-cup machines do the entire thing for you, including the milk.

You just press the button and walk away with your coffee. Well, almost, you need to fill the milk carafe, clean it after, and so on.

If you value convenience over anything else, and you're not the kind of person who wants to tinker with things in order to get the absolute best-tasting shot possible, then a bean-to-cup coffee machine may be a better match for you.

If you want a more authentic home espresso-making experience, then keep reading, as the DeLonghi Dedica is a semi-automatic traditional espresso machine.

What is a Home Barista?

Before we move on, I think it's essential that potential traditional espresso machine users understand that using a machine like this means becoming a home barista, at least to some degree.

A home barista is someone who has developed the skills to produce coffee shop-quality coffee at home.

It requires such skills to use a traditional espresso machine. It's not like using a Nespresso machine or a bean-to-cup machine.

With that being said, it doesn't require quite the same level of home barista skills to use a lower-cost, domestic espresso machine with pressure baskets. 

I'll explain this more shortly, but in brief, if you're happy with “OK” espresso, you may be more than satisfied with the results you'll get from this kind of machine, with a relatively low learning curve. 

But if you want “great” espresso, not just “OK”, then you'll need to forget pressured baskets, and you'll need to develop some skills – but more on this shortly.

Domestic Espresso Machines Vs Home Barista Espresso Machines

Lower-cost, domestic espresso machines differ from the higher-cost machines designed for the home barista market.

While domestic machines cost around £100-£200, home barista machines typically start from around £400-£500 and above.

It's not just a difference in looks or materials; they're very different things. 

The main difference is that domestic lower-cost machines usually have:

  • 15 bar pumps
  • No over-pressure valve – so they pull shots at 15 bars, some even at 19
  • Thermoblock heaters 
  • No solenoid valve
  • Pressured portafilter baskets.
  • Panarello steam wands

If you're interested in the slightly more technical side of things, then read the following section – if you're really not ;-), just scroll down to the “DeLonghi Dedica Coffee Machine Review” text below.

Bars of pressure refer to the amount of pressure the machine uses to extract the shot. 

The standard commercial espresso pressure is 9 bars, equivalent to approximately 130 PSI. Most lower-cost machines extract at 15 bars, almost 220 PSI. 

This higher pressure isn't an advantage, as some marketing blurb would have you believe.

It's mainly because fitting a lower-pressure pump or an overpressure valve to limit the pressure to 9 bars would make it a more expensive machine to build.

Thermoblocks are usually used in lower-cost espresso machines as opposed to brew boilers.

Thermoblocks are on-demand water heaters, think combi boiler vs tank-fed boiler.

Note I say thermoblock and not thermocoil.

Some higher-end machines, now including some of the Sage machines, feature thermocoils, which offer a slight improvement over the thermoblock in terms of performance and longevity. However, many cheaper domestic espresso machines still use thermoblocks.

Anyway, back to the dedication, as that's why you are here!

Regarding the solenoid valve, both home barista espresso machines and commercial machines feature this function to drain excess moisture from the coffee puck after pulling the shot, leaving a dry puck to be knocked out of the portafilter. 

Pressure portafilter baskets are typically used with lower-cost espresso machines, rather than standard espresso filter baskets. 

A filter basket is basically a shallow metal cup with holes in the bottom.

The coffee is placed into the filter basket, the basket is inserted into the portafilter, the portafilter is locked into the group head, and water is then pushed into the basket, where the espresso is extracted. 

To properly extract espresso using a standard basket, you need an espresso-capable grinder and some home barista skills. 

Pressured baskets negate the need for both of the above. You can simply put pre-ground coffee in them, or use coffee ground via a less expensive coffee grinder, which wouldn't be suitable for grinding to be used with standard baskets.

Espresso pulled this way can actually look fairly good, as these baskets tend to do a good job of creating espresso that appears to have thick crema, which is usually the sign of a well-extracted shot of espresso.

The problem is, though, that although these baskets do quite a good job visually, they don't quite do the same job when it comes to taste. 

Not all pressured baskets are the same; some do a better job than others, and some can be converted into standard basket machines. However, nearly all espresso machines under £200 come as pressure-brew machines.

DeLonghi Dedica Coffee Machine Review

Okay, so if you're still reading at this point, I'll assume that you've decided you want a traditional espresso machine, not a bean-to-cup coffee machine.

Also, I'm guessing that you either don't currently have the budget for an entry-level home barista machine, or can't justify the cost (to yourself, or to your better half), and spending the best part of a couple of hundred quid is about your limit at the moment. 

If the above is correct, then unless you opt for a used espresso machine, I don't think there's really anything else in this price range that would be a better option than the Delonghi Dedica, and in particular, the Dedica EC685, which is the latest Dedica model. 

I'll discuss the features first, and then share my thoughts on this machine after using it.

Size & Weight:

14.9 x 33 x 30.3 cm. 4.2 Kg

The Best Bits:

  • It's fairly simple to turn this into a home barista espresso machine.
  • It's capable of producing latte art-worthy milk texture, even if you choose to use the Panarello wand.
  • Removable drip tray for using taller cups.
  • A steam wand can be used as a hot water tap.
  • Adjustable brew temperature.

The Worst Bits:

  • I think it's slightly overpriced at full RRP.
  • Erm – actually, nothing else I can think of.

I bought the DeLonghi Dedica espresso machine to use it for my YouTube review. 

If you watch that video, you'll see that overall, I was really quite impressed with the Dedica. 

Aside from being a cheaper 15-bar pressure basket domestic espresso machine, which we already know, there's really nothing much that I can say negatively about this machine. 

The only thing I would say is that I think it could be a bit cheaper.

I think this is the very best espresso machine you can get at this price, all things considered. But it's still a thermoblock machine with a 15-bar pump; I can't help thinking it should be about fifty quid cheaper. 

However, to be fair, it's often available at this price, and when it is, I think it's a decent value for money.

There's plenty of good stuff to say about this little espresso machine, though. 

The first thing to say is that although you can't mod the pressure (as far as I'm aware) you can really easily switch to a standard basket.

If you watch the video above, you'll see that I bought a bottomless portafilter with a standard basket – it's literally as easy as that, at a cost of about £ 20.

You can do it even cheaper than this by just swapping out the basket and keeping the portafilter, but you might need to bend the lip of the basket slightly to get it to fit, so buying the bottomless portafilter with the standard basket seemed the easiest way to do it, plus the fact that bottomless baskets are cool.

Not only are bottomless or naked portafilters cool, they're also useful, as they tell you visually how well you've done at shot prep. If you're careful with dosing, distribution, and tamping, you should see that you've avoided channelling. 

Again, if you watch the video above, you'll see what channelling looks like – jets of water spraying out at random angles through the basket. 

Pulling shots at 15 bars isn't great for channelling. You'll need to work harder with puck prep to avoid it than if you were using a 9-bar machine. I'll discuss this further shortly.

The DeLonghi Dedica does feature automatic preinfusion, though, and this will help to some degree in this regard. 

Preinfusion refers to the process of introducing some water to the coffee puck in the basket first, typically under low pressure, before the water is introduced under higher pressure.

This means the puck is already wet before pressured water hits the puck, which helps prevent channelling. 

I can't find any information on whether this is actually low-pressure pre-infusion. I suspect it may just be a program that blasts a smaller amount of water at the puck of coffee at full pressure, then pauses before continuing.

“What the chuff is channelling?” I hear you say.

Channelling occurs when water finds channels through the puck of coffee in the basket, resulting in uneven extraction and a bad-tasting coffee.

You can also adjust the brew temperature, a feature typically reserved for more expensive espresso machines.

This is also the first time I've ever been impressed by a Panarello steam wand!

Panarello wands are the thick sheaths that fit onto a steam wand to enable anyone to steam milk. The problem is that Panarello wands usually only produce thick, spoonable froth. 

If this is what you want, then great. However, I would usually remove the Panarello on these kinds of machines and use the exposed steam pipe as a standard steam wand. 

You can do this with the DeLonghi Dedica (just need to cable tie the rubber pipe on or it'll pop off and splash you with milk), but you can also use the Panarello to make really decent milk texture!

All you do is start off with the Panarello at the “hot water” setting for a few seconds while attempting to create a vortex in your milk jug.

Then, change the setting to “cappuccino” to start aerating the milk. After approximately 40 seconds, depending on how stiff you want your foam, change it back to the hot water setting and stop steaming once you reach your desired milk temperature. 

This actually produces latte art worthy textured milk! As you'll see in my tutorial below.

This significantly reduces the learning curve for anyone who wants a nicer milk texture without developing the skill.

Kev's Guide to Best Espresso With The DeLonghi Dedica

If you've decided to go for the Dedica, or if you already have it and you found your way here by googling for tips and tricks to get better results with your DeLonghi Dedica, here's my guide to getting the best possible results with your machine.

1: Use great coffee

If you want great coffee out, you have to put great coffee in. 

If you're buying coffee beans in bags from a supermarket, most of the time (there are exceptions) this isn't going to be great quality coffee.

In fact, most of the time, what you're buying is stale (to some degree) commodity coffee beans that were roasted to within an inch of charcoal in order to achieve batch consistency.

You should use freshly roasted, high-quality coffee beans. 

If you're planning to use only the pressure baskets that come with the Dedica and aren't interested in the home barista hobby, simply scroll past the following nine tips below and proceed to the conclusion.

2: Use a good burr grinder

Burr grinders are essential for espresso; please don't bother trying to use a blade grinder.

You'll likely get limited results using a very inexpensive grinder, such as the DeLonghi KG79 or Krups Expert. I'd advise going for the Iberital MC2, Lelit Fred, Sage Dose Control Pro, Sage Smart Grinder Pro, Baratza Sette, or one of the Eureka Mignon grinders. 

The above range from £125 to £300 new, but have a look at eBay, as these grinders do often come up used. 

If you're going for a used grinder, though, also consider ex-commercial grinders, in particular, the Mazzer Super Jolly – these are serious commercial grinders. 

They usually come with a doser, which is overkill for home use. However, there are popular mods for this grinder that make it doserless and also turn it into a single-dose grinder.

 

3: Standard Baskets

As I mentioned earlier, you'll need to use standard baskets if you want to make the best possible espresso via your Delonghi Dedica. 

You can obtain a high-quality competition basket, similar to this one, and make slight modifications to your Delonghi portafilter to ensure it fits. You'll also need to bend the lip of the basket slightly with pliers and then hammer it into shape. 

Alternatively, you can buy a bottomless portafilter, like this one (which I have), which comes with a standard basket; this way, no DIY is required. 

It's worth noting that the standard that comes with the bottomless portafilter may not be as good as the IMS competition basket above. For optimal results, you may want to either modify the IMS basket to fit your Delonghi portafilter or purchase both the bottomless portafilter and the IMS basket.

I'm not entirely sure if the IMS basket fits the bottomless portafilter without any modifications, but I've purchased it, and I'll update this post once I've confirmed.

You could also use your DeLonghi basket with the IMS basket above and cut away the bottom part of the portafilter (carefully) to create a bottomless portafilter.

4: Take Time to Dial in the Grind

Dialling in means finding the correct grind size for the beans you're using. I favour a one-to-two extraction, usually. For example, if you're dosing your double basket with 14g, I'd look for 28 grams of espresso in 28-32 seconds. Feel free to experiment with other extractions and other recipes, of course. If the espresso is flowing faster than you intended, you need to adjust the grind to be finer. If the espresso is flowing slower than you're aiming for, you need to adjust the grind to be coarser.

When you're getting started, I'd recommend buying your coffee by the kilo or purchasing multiple bags of the same 250g beans from the same roaster and roast batch.

This way, you'll have plenty of coffee to work with while you perfect things. It's very annoying when you just get dialled in, and then you run out of beans! You need to dial in for each new bag of beans you buy, you see, so it makes sense to buy plenty of the same freshly roasted beans.

You can't properly dial in if you don't weigh the ground coffee and the espresso in the cup, so you'll need a brew scale.

The Timemore black mirror scales above are probably the best mid-priced brew scales (in my humble opinion, and I've used a few).  

If you think this is a lot to pay for a brew scales (and it's actually not, comparatively speaking), then see these. They won't be as responsive, they'll require batteries rather than being rechargeable, and they won't be as pleasant to use, but they're inexpensive, and they'll do the job.

5: Proper Puck Prep

Remember, I mentioned earlier that we need to avoid channeling?

Puck prep is how we strive to minimise channeling while ensuring the best possible extraction. 

Puck prep consists of dosing, distributing, and tamping. 

Dosing means using the correct dose for the basket you're using. I'm going to experiment with this, but depending on the basket you're using, I suspect it'll be around 14g. The IMS competition basket I mentioned is made to use 14 grams. 

Distributing means ensuring an even distribution of the coffee in the basket. I'd recommend the WDT method for this, which essentially involves using a thin, pointed object (such as a needle or straightened paper clip) to gently pass through the puck of coffee, breaking up all the clumps. 

If you're going to do this properl,y you really need to spend a bit of time on it if you're using a single needle or paperclip, but there are tools you can use so that you're using multiple pointy things at the same time, to speed things up.

You can buy expensive tools for this if you prefer, but I recommend simply getting a keycap puller like this and cutting the ends off or inserting needles into a cork. I'll create a video on this in the near future, as many of the videos I've seen about this method aren't great.

6: Level Tamp

The most important thing about tamping your coffee is to achieve a level tamp.

Don't worry too much about the tamping pressure; just tamp at a pressure that is fairly easy to replicate each time. Pay the most attention to ensure that the surface of the coffee puck is level. 

7: Do Not Disturb

Once you've distributed and tamped, be careful not to bang the portafilter when locking it into the group; if you're not careful, you can break the puck, making channelling inevitable.

8: Turn up the Heat

Especially if you're going to be using light to medium roasted beans and not medium to dark, I'd recommend using the hottest shot setting on the Dedica.

To do this, hold the steam button for 10 seconds to enter the settings menu.

Then, press the single-shot button, and the lights will start flashing. Press the steam button to select high temperature (the single-shot button is low temp, the double-shot button is medium).

9: Flush the Group & Portafilter

Before each shot, pull at least a single double shot of water through the portafilter – I'd recommend two double shots worth for the best results.

Just place your cup on the drip tray so you can warm it up at the same time.

10: Keep Your Eyes on it.

If you're using a bottomless portafilter, keep a close eye on the shot.

What you don't want to see is “spurters” or multiple streams of espresso, both of which are signs of channelling.

 

 

What you'd hope to see from a well-extracted shot is a single flow of espresso forming towards the centre of the basket, with nice-looking tiger striping.

        Thank you

        Reply
        1. Kev says:
          at

          Hi Susan :-).
          Americano is simply espresso mixed with hot water. So just make your espresso, then top up with hot water, taste, and if it’s too strong, add more hot water. I’d start off at about 1:1 espresso to water, and go from there. Personally I tend to end up quite big on water to espresso for Americano, like 1 part espresso to 4 parts water, some people like it 1:1, some even more dilute such as 1:6 or higher, it depends on the coffee too. So I’d say just experiment and see what works for your coffee and your taste buds :-).
